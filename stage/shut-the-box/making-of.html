How I built Shut the Box

Joel Dare · 2026-08-30

If you want a copy of Shut the Box that opens instantly on your phone, keeps nothing on a server, and never asks you to sign in, that is what this is: one page you can play right now. Here is how it got made, and why it is so small.

1. It is a board, not a referee

A wooden Shut the Box does not check your arithmetic or announce that the game is over. You do. I kept that. The page rolls two dice and lets you shut tiles; it does not verify that the tiles you shut add up to your roll, and it never tells you that you are stuck. The only rule it enforces is the real one from the pub: you cannot roll again until you have shut at least one tile. Everything else is on the honor system, the same as it is with a physical box on a table.

2. One file, no build step

The whole game is a single index.html with its CSS and JavaScript inline. No framework, no bundler, no npm install, nothing to compile. It is served as static files, so there is no backend to patch and nothing to fall over. Your scores, your best, and your daily streak live in your browser's localStorage and are never sent anywhere. The only network call is a privacy-preserving page-view counter that sees a URL and a timestamp, nothing else.

3. Challenge a friend, still with no server

After you finish a round, the page works out your best score for the day and offers a Challenge a friend button. It builds a message like “My best Shut the Box score today was 3. Can you beat it?” and a link that carries a single number: ?best=3. Open that link and a small “Beat 3” banner sits above the board while play stays exactly the same. When you finish a round it tells you whether you beat the number and offers to send one back. There is no account, no database of challenges, and no tracking pixel. The score is a friendly claim, the way it would be if your friend just told you across the table.
